Methodology

What we do, why, and how

The framework is Professor Dimitrios Thomakos's: Ars Prognostica — The Origins, Evolution, and Formalization of Prediction (2026) and Intelligence Without Wisdom — Practical Judgment and the Epistemology of AI Risk Claims (2026), both published at the Cybernetics and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory of the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens. The Oracle Hunters applies it; any error in the application is ours. Each step below says what we do, why, and how.

Also from the laboratory: Symbiosis & AI Risk - Dimitrios Thomakos, CAIL, 8 March 2026.

One method with ATHENA: RIM-1

The Oracle Hunters and ATHENA's Forecast Desk use one method, the ATHENA Risk Intelligence Method (RIM-1), approved by Professor Dimitrios Thomakos. Its top layer is Ars Prognostica: what a prediction is about, and whether it can be held to account. ATHENA files a call of its own only when it would score here as fully testable and free of hedging - a defined event, a date, a stated likelihood, a measurable outcome, and no “could”, “might” or “maybe”. Every statement on this site is marked meets or below that standard.

Every statement that carries a likelihood is also read as the call it implies: Yes when the words put it above 50%, No below, with the confidence of that call and its classical term - eikos (probable), hōs epi to poly (for the most part), anankaion (as good as necessary). Speakers and ATHENA are scored by the same rules, so their records can be compared directly.

The nine words - how every statement is rated

Where English has one word, “prediction”, the Greeks had a family of words, each naming a different way of knowing the future. Every statement on the record is rated with the word that fits it. What we use: the vocabulary only. The mathematics in the monograph and in the companion (Bayes' rule, entropy, the optimal-ambivalence formula, the minimax trade-off, quantum probability, the POMDP) is there to understand the argument; it is not used to rate statements. How: fixed word rules, applied in this order, on the classification already made in steps 3-4.

Other languages: statements in Spanish, Turkish, Portuguese, German, French, Russian, Chinese and some thirty other languages are translated offline into English (Argos, on our own computer - nothing is sent anywhere) and then rated by the same rules. Every translated statement says so and links to the original words. Leaders are translated first.

Order of the rules: a statement that weighs conditions and risks is phronesis; one that interprets a leader's will is propheteia; a forecast resting on visible signs is problepsis; a commitment is chresmodosia when the institution speaks with no named speaker, otherwise proanangelia; a scenario or a call to prepare is pronoia; an educated guess is eikasia; a forecast resting on authority alone is manteia; a forecast inferred from data is prognosis. The word judges the sentence, never the person. Every statement's evidence page says which rule applied.

1. Collecting

What: speeches and statements from official, primary sources only (live list): central banks' own archives (ECB since 1997, Federal Reserve since 2006, every central bank's speeches via the BIS since 1996), governments (GOV.UK, the European Commission, the White House, the Greek Prime Minister's office and Ministry of Finance, foreign ministries), international bodies and AI companies. Why: a quote from a news report can be shortened or paraphrased; the official text cannot be disputed by the speaker. How: each document keeps the time it was said and the time we captured it (UTC) and a fingerprint of its text. A speech that reaches us through two official channels is stored once.

2. Choosing sentences

What: up to 6 sentences per document that speak about the future. Why: a speech is mostly description; the record is about claims that can turn out right or wrong. How: rules look for future tense, expectations, projections, deadlines and likelihoods; procedural sentences (“I will now turn to…”) are dropped. Only forecasts, commitments and scenarios with at least two of the four checkable elements - and all AI-risk statements - enter the public ledger; vaguer remarks stay in the archive.

3. Classifying

Kind of claim (six speech acts): forecast, scenario, speculation, commitment, call for action, or mixed. What it needs as evidence (four tiers): possibility, mechanism, forecast, policy. How the speaker claims to know (three axes): based on data (empirical), educated guess (abductive), stated on authority (oracular). Why: a scenario is not a forecast and a forecast is not a mandate to act - treating them alike is the commonest mistake in public debate. How: published rules (rules-2.0, in method.json); the reasons for each label are shown on every statement's evidence page.

4. Can it be checked?

Four yes/no questions: is the event named, is there a firm deadline, is a likelihood given, is there a measurable figure? Four of four: can be checked. Two or three: partly. Vague time frames (“over time”, “in the medium term”) do not count as deadlines.

5. Ambiguity

A practical version of the ambivalence function of Ars Prognostica (the entropy over the readings a statement allows): readings grow with hedging words and with each missing element; ambiguity = log₂(readings), scaled to 0-100. It is a proxy, published so anyone can recompute it.

7. Checking the outcome

Institutional forecasts (numbers with dates) are compared automatically with official outcomes - see the scoreboard. Statements by people: when the deadline passes, the program looks up the official annual figure (World Bank open data: inflation, growth, unemployment, by country) and settles it automatically with its arithmetic - see the checking rules below. Earlier rule (until 14 Nov 2026): an editor approved every verdict about a person; nothing of that kind is published automatically. Why: a sentence can be misread by a rule, and a wrong verdict about a named person is not acceptable.

8. Scoring

Report cards show plain counts. Calibration uses the Brier score - the mean of (likelihood − outcome)², a proper scoring rule, so it rewards honest likelihoods rather than confident-sounding ones - and appears only after 10 checked forecasts.

9. Rules we keep

Primary sources only · the same tests for optimists and pessimists, every party, every country · the original words always next to their label · labels judge the claim, never the person · every change logged · a right of reply · short excerpts with a link to the full source · daily seals so the record cannot be quietly rewritten.

10. Known limits

The rules read English and Greek well; statements in other languages are archived but mostly not yet classified. Official figures can differ in definition from the one a speaker had in mind (national CPI versus HICP, for example); the figure used is always shown. Ministries without a named speaker are scored as institutions. History before our first capture is sealed on the day we read it.

11. The checking rules (version 3, automatic)

Every statement whose deadline has passed is read with these fixed rules and settled without a human step. The rules prefer saying nothing to saying something wrong about a named person.

  • Only headline measures: consumer-price inflation, real GDP growth and the unemployment rate, as annual figures. A sentence about core inflation, potential growth, GNP or GNI, wages, credit, deficits, debt, company revenue, a quarter, a month, a fiscal year or the end of a year is not settled this way - the annual figure would not be a fair test.
  • Only the speaker's own forecast: sentences that quote a survey, analysts, markets, the IMF, the OECD, the World Bank or a news agency are left out.
  • The place named in the sentence: if the sentence names one country or the euro area, that is what is checked; if it names none, the speaker's own country; if it names several, or speaks of the world or a region, it is left out.
  • Each number with its own year: “1.9% this year and 2.0% next year” are two claims; the one that falls due is checked. A range is checked as a range.
  • Verdicts: within 0.5 percentage points - on target; within 1.0 - close; further - missed. “Below” and “above” are checked as stated. “Will rise / fall” is checked against the change from the year before; a change smaller than 0.1 points counts as close.
  • Published automatically, labelled “automatic check against official data”, with the figure, its source and the arithmetic. A correction can be requested on the corrections page; every change is logged.
  • Known limit: the official annual figure can differ in definition from the one the speaker had in mind (national CPI versus HICP; the European Union's membership changed over time). The figure used is always shown.
