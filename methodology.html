Methodology

The framework is Professor Dimitrios Thomakos's: Ars Prognostica — The Origins, Evolution, and Formalization of Prediction (2026) and Intelligence Without Wisdom — Practical Judgment and the Epistemology of AI Risk Claims (2026). The Oracle Hunters applies it; any error in the application is ours. Each step below says what we do, why, and how.

1. Collecting

What: speeches and statements from official, primary sources only (live list): central banks' own archives (ECB since 1997, Federal Reserve since 2006, every central bank's speeches via the BIS since 1996), governments (GOV.UK, the European Commission, the White House, the Greek Prime Minister's office and Ministry of Finance, foreign ministries), international bodies and AI companies. Why: a quote from a news report can be shortened or paraphrased; the official text cannot be disputed by the speaker. How: each document keeps the time it was said and the time we captured it (UTC) and a fingerprint of its text. A speech that reaches us through two official channels is stored once.

2. Choosing sentences

What: up to 6 sentences per document that speak about the future. Why: a speech is mostly description; the record is about claims that can turn out right or wrong. How: rules look for future tense, expectations, projections, deadlines and likelihoods; procedural sentences (“I will now turn to…”) are dropped. Only forecasts, commitments and scenarios with at least two of the four checkable elements - and all AI-risk statements - enter the public ledger; vaguer remarks stay in the archive.

3. Classifying

Kind of claim (six speech acts): forecast, scenario, speculation, commitment, call for action, or mixed. What it needs as evidence (four tiers): possibility, mechanism, forecast, policy. How the speaker claims to know (three axes): based on data (empirical), educated guess (abductive), stated on authority (oracular). Why: a scenario is not a forecast and a forecast is not a mandate to act - treating them alike is the commonest mistake in public debate. How: published rules (rules-2.0, in method.json); the reasons for each label are shown on every statement's evidence page.

4. Can it be checked?

Four yes/no questions: is the event named, is there a firm deadline, is a likelihood given, is there a measurable figure? Four of four: can be checked. Two or three: partly. Vague time frames (“over time”, “in the medium term”) do not count as deadlines.

5. Ambiguity

A practical version of the ambivalence function of Ars Prognostica (the entropy over the readings a statement allows): readings grow with hedging words and with each missing element; ambiguity = log₂(readings), scaled to 0-100. It is a proxy, published so anyone can recompute it.

7. Checking the outcome

Institutional forecasts (numbers with dates) are compared automatically with official outcomes - see the scoreboard. Statements by people: when the deadline passes, the program looks up the official annual figure (World Bank open data: inflation, growth, unemployment, by country) and proposes a verdict with its arithmetic - within 0.5 percentage points: came true; within 1.0: partly; for “will rise / fall”: the direction of the year-on-year change. An editor approves every verdict about a person; nothing of that kind is published automatically. Why: a sentence can be misread by a rule, and a wrong verdict about a named person is not acceptable.

8. Scoring

Report cards show plain counts. Calibration uses the Brier score - the mean of (likelihood − outcome)², a proper scoring rule, so it rewards honest likelihoods rather than confident-sounding ones - and appears only after 10 checked forecasts.

9. Rules we keep

Primary sources only · the same tests for optimists and pessimists, every party, every country · the original words always next to their label · labels judge the claim, never the person · every change logged · a right of reply · short excerpts with a link to the full source · daily seals so the record cannot be quietly rewritten.

10. Known limits

The rules read English and Greek well; statements in other languages are archived but mostly not yet classified. Official figures can differ in definition from the one a speaker had in mind (national CPI versus HICP, for example); the figure used is always shown. Ministries without a named speaker are scored as institutions. History before our first capture is sealed on the day we read it.
